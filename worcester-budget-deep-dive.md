# City of Worcester, MA — Budget Deep Dive & 5/10/20-Year Projections

*Prepared June 2026. Figures FY25–FY26 are sourced from official City of Worcester documents, the Worcester Regional Research Bureau (WRRB), the Worcester Retirement System ACFR, the FY26 Annual Capital Budget, MA DOR Division of Local Services, and the May 2024 Moody's credit opinion (see Sources). All claims below were cross-checked and adversarially verified. The 10- and 20-year projections are **a model built on the verified FY26 base** — no primary source publishes horizons beyond ~5 years, so those are clearly labeled as extrapolation.*

---

## 1. Executive summary

Worcester runs a roughly **$948M general operating budget (FY26)**, up 3.4% from $893M in FY25. The budget is structurally healthy today — balanced by law, investment-grade rated (Moody's **Aa3** / Fitch **AA** / S&P **AA-**, all stable), and disciplined on pensions — but it sits on a slow-moving structural tension:

- **Revenue is ~50% locally raised / ~50% state aid.** The local half (property tax) is capped by **Proposition 2½** at ~2.5% + new growth. The state half is dominated by **Chapter 70** education aid, whose generous **Student Opportunity Act (SOA)** phase-in *ends in FY26*.
- **Spending is dominated by education (~61%)**, which is also the fastest-growing line (+5.3% FY25, +6.1% FY26). 86% of the school budget is salaries and benefits — hard to compress.
- **The pension is a success story**: ~79% funded and on an accelerated schedule to be *fully funded by ~FY2033-34*, well ahead of the state's 2040 mandate. After full funding, a ~$25M annual appropriation frees up.
- **OPEB (retiree health care) is the dominant long-term risk**: a **~$1.16B liability that is ~96% unfunded** (~$47M in trust), funded pay-as-you-go (~$25M/yr) with **no legal requirement to ever fully fund it.**

The core dynamic for projections: **cost-to-maintain-services grows ~4.5%/yr while constrained revenue grows ~3.4%/yr.** That ~1 point/yr gap is invisible in any single year but compounds into a material structural gap over 10–20 years.

## 2. Current budget structure (verified)

### 2.1 Top line

| | FY24 | FY25 | FY26 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| General operating budget | $867M | $893.0M | ~$948M |
| YoY growth | — | +3.0% | +3.4% |

> Note: a separate "~$1B" figure sometimes cited is *all-funds* spending including the water/sewer **enterprise funds**; the general operating budget is ~$948M.

### 2.2 Revenue mix (~50% local / ~50% state)

| Source | FY25 | FY25 % | FY26 | FY26 % |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Property tax (Prop 2½ levy) | $396M | 44.4% | $413.8M | 43.6% |
| State education aid (mostly Chapter 70) | $387M | 43.4% | $420.6M | 44.4% |
| Local receipts | $57M | 6.3% | $59.1M | 6.2% |
| General state aid (UGGA) | $53M | 5.9% | $54.4M | 5.7% |

The two big levers — property tax and Chapter 70 — are each ~44% of revenue. One is capped by Prop 2½; the other is set on Beacon Hill. **Worcester controls less than half its own revenue.**

### 2.3 Expenditure mix

| Category | FY25 | FY25 % | FY26 | FY26 % | FY26 growth |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Education | $533M | 60% | $574M | 61% | +6.1% (SOA) |
| City services | $195M | 22% | $210M | 22% | ~+4% |
| Fixed costs (pensions, debt, health ins.) | $165M | 18% | $163M | 17% | +6.5% |

The FY25 *decline* in fixed costs (-5.4%) was partly reduced OPEB contributions and lower debt payments — i.e., flexibility was found by easing on the least-mandated items, which is exactly where future stress lands.

## 3. The cost drivers

### 3.1 Worcester Public Schools — the engine

- FY25 WPS **General Fund** budget: **$485.7M** (+$23.8M over FY24); up nearly **$113M (+30%) over five years**.
- FY26 WPS adopted budget (general + special revenue): **$586.3M**, +6.1%.
- **86% of the WPS budget is salaries and benefits.**
- FY26 is the **final year of the SOA phase-in.** After this, the foundation-budget buffer that drove large state-aid increases goes away — minimum aid increases revert to far smaller amounts.
- The City projects **~$26M in additional Required Local Contributions** to education over the next five years — money the *city* must put in regardless of state aid.

**Why this matters for projections:** education is simultaneously the largest line, the fastest-growing line, and the most personnel-heavy (hardest to cut). After FY26 the state-aid tailwind weakens while the local-contribution obligation keeps climbing.

### 3.2 Pension — on a credible glide path

- **Worcester Retirement System** (the City system, distinct from the separate *Worcester Regional* system).
- Net pension liability **$385.0M** as of 12/31/24 (TPL $1,812.3M − assets $1,427.3M); funded **78.8% (fair value) / 79.1% (actuarial)**. On the 1/1/24 valuation the UAAL was **$411.6M** (the $412M vs $385M difference is just valuation date/basis).
- **Accelerated funding:** legally required to assume full funding by 2040, but Worcester committed to **full funding by ~FY2033-34.** Appropriations rise ~3.5%/yr through FY2033, then **drop ~50% in FY2034** once the liability is retired.
- This is a genuine strength — most MA cities are not this far ahead.

### 3.3 OPEB — the elephant

- Liability **~$1.16B** (6/30/24), against an irrevocable OPEB Trust (created FY20) holding only **~$47.4M** → **~96% unfunded.**
- Funded **pay-as-you-go at ~$25M/yr**; **no statutory full-funding mandate** (unlike pensions).
- Historical trajectory: WRRB pegged the unfunded OPEB liability at **$727M in 2015**, projected to pass **$1B by 2023** and **$2B by 2042** absent advance funding — and the $1.16B figure shows that trajectory is roughly on track.
- **This is the single largest unaddressed claim on Worcester's future budgets.** The plan is essentially "redirect pension dollars to OPEB after ~FY2034," but that reallocation is not yet formally locked in.

### 3.4 Debt & capital — manageable, with built-in relief

- Total debt **$1.05B** (6/30/24): $1,013.2M GO bonds + $40.6M BANs. After **$646.6M self-supporting deductions** (enterprise/sewer etc.), **net direct debt = $407.2M.**
- FY26 **Capital Improvement Plan**: $113.1M total annual borrowing / $158.9M new authorization ($42.95M tax-levy, $49.7M enterprise/self-funded, $9.4M WPS-MSBA, ~$32M potential grants).
- **Key tailwind:** existing tax-levy debt service is scheduled to *fall* from **$43.3M (FY26) → $26.8M (FY30)**, and total existing debt service from **$101.2M → $74.3M** — creating room to absorb new borrowing without spiking the levy. *(This decline schedule was the one claim that drew a dissenting verification vote, though the figures matched the primary CIP document verbatim — treat as solid but city-projected.)*
- **Ratings:** Moody's Aa3, Fitch AA (an upgrade), S&P AA- (an upgrade) — all **stable outlooks** as of Feb 2024.

## 4. The structural mechanics (why a gap forms)

```
        REVENUE (capped/exogenous)              SPENDING (mandated/sticky)
  ┌─────────────────────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Property tax  44%  → Prop 2½     │    │ Education     61%  → +5-6%/yr    │
  │   ≈ +2.5% + new growth (~4%)     │    │   86% salaries/benefits         │
  │ Chapter 70    44%  → SOA ENDS    │    │ City services 22%  → ~+3.5%/yr  │
  │   FY26; reverts to min. aid      │    │ Fixed costs   17%  → pension ↓   │
  │ Local/UGGA    12%  → ~+2-3%/yr   │    │   after FY34, OPEB ↑, health ↑  │
  └─────────────────────────────────┘    └─────────────────────────────────┘
        blended ≈ +3.4%/yr                       blended ≈ +4.5%/yr
                         └──────────  ~1.1 pt/yr gap  ──────────┘
                                  (compounds over time)
```

Each year individually is balanced by law (Massachusetts cities cannot adopt a deficit budget). The "gap" below is therefore not a literal deficit — it's the **annual amount that must be closed every year** through some combination of: service cuts, drawing on reserves, new growth above trend, a Prop 2½ override, or stronger-than-modeled state aid.

## 5. Projections — 5 / 10 / 20 years

> **Methodology & honesty note.** These are *my* extrapolations from the verified FY26 base ($948M), not figures from any city document (primary sources stop at a 5-year debt-service schedule). I model a blended revenue growth and a blended "cost-to-maintain" growth, then show the gap. Assumptions are stated so you can flex them. Real outcomes depend heavily on state aid, health-insurance inflation, collective-bargaining settlements, and whether voters approve overrides.

### 5.1 Base-case assumptions

| Revenue component | Weight | Growth | Rationale |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Property tax | 43.6% | 4.0% | 2.5% Prop 2½ + ~1.5% new growth |
| State education aid | 44.4% | 3.0% | SOA ends FY26; reverts toward minimum-aid/inflation |
| Local receipts | 6.2% | 3.0% | economy-linked |
| General state aid (UGGA) | 5.7% | 2.0% | historically lags |
| **Blended revenue** | | **≈ 3.4%/yr** | |

| Spending component | Weight | Growth | Rationale |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Education | 60.5% | 5.0% | personnel/benefits + required local contribution |
| City services | 22.2% | 3.5% | wages + inflation |
| Fixed costs | 17.2% | 4.5% | pension +3.5% then drop FY34; health ins. +6-7%; OPEB pressure |
| **Blended cost-to-maintain** | | **≈ 4.6%/yr** | |

### 5.2 Base case — revenue vs. cost-to-maintain

| Horizon | Revenue | Cost-to-maintain | Annual structural gap | Gap as % of budget |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **FY26 (base)** | $948M | $948M | $0 | balanced |
| **5-yr — FY31** | ~$1,120M | ~$1,187M | **~$67M** | ~5.6% |
| **10-yr — FY36** | ~$1,324M | ~$1,486M | **~$162M** | ~10.9% |
| **20-yr — FY46** | ~$1,850M | ~$2,330M | **~$480M** | ~20.6% |

Interpretation: under "current trend continues," Worcester faces a **~$67M annual gap within 5 years (~5-6% of budget)** widening to a **~$160M gap at 10 years** and a **~$480M gap at 20 years.** These are amounts that must be closed each year — cumulatively over 20 years the gap-closing effort runs into the **billions** if nothing changes structurally.

### 5.3 Scenario range

| | Optimistic | Base | Stress |
|---|---|---|---|
| State aid growth | 4.5% (state holds Ch.70 generous) | 3.0% | 1.0% (recession/level-funding) |
| New growth (tax) | 2.5% | 1.5% | 0.5% |
| Health-ins / benefit inflation | 4% | 6% | 8% |
| **~5-yr annual gap (FY31)** | **~$10-20M** (manageable) | **~$67M** | **~$130M+** |
| **~10-yr annual gap (FY36)** | **~$40M** | **~$162M** | **~$330M+** |
| **20-yr posture** | gap stabilizes ~5-7% of budget; closable with overrides + new growth | persistent 10-20% structural gap | recurring override dependence / service retrenchment |

### 5.4 Two structural offsets that improve the picture (and are *not* fully in the base model)

1. **Pension cliff relief (~FY2034).** When the pension hits full funding, the appropriation drops ~50% — roughly **$20-25M/yr freed.** If redeployed to OPEB, this is the single most important lever Worcester holds. *Open question: this redeployment is not yet formally committed.*
2. **Declining existing debt service (FY26→FY30).** Tax-levy debt service falling $43.3M→$26.8M frees ~$16M of levy capacity over five years, partially absorbing new CIP borrowing.

Together these are worth on the order of **$35-45M/yr by the mid-2030s** — enough to offset roughly half the base-case 5-year gap *if* captured and not spent elsewhere.

### 5.5 OPEB as its own 20-year line

The $1.16B OPEB liability is the wildcard. To actually fund it (vs. pay-go) on, say, a 25-30 year amortization would require ramping the annual contribution from ~$25M today toward **$60-90M/yr** — a $35-65M/yr incremental cost that the base operating model above does **not** include. The realistic path: redirect the freed pension dollars (~$25M) starting ~FY2034 and let the trust compound, which materially slows but does not eliminate the liability over 20 years. Left entirely unfunded, the liability trends toward the **~$2B by 2042** that WRRB warned about a decade ago.

## 6. Key long-term risks (ranked)

1. **OPEB (~$1.16B, ~96% unfunded, no mandate).** The largest unaddressed obligation. Each year of pay-go-only defers a growing bill onto future budgets.
2. **State-aid dependence post-SOA.** With ~44% of revenue from state education aid and the SOA tailwind ending in FY26, Worcester is highly exposed to Beacon Hill's appetite for Chapter 70 — and to any state-revenue downturn.
3. **Education/benefit cost growth vs. Prop 2½.** Salaries + health insurance (86% of WPS) compound at 5-7% against a ~4% levy cap. This is the engine of the structural gap.
4. **Health-insurance inflation** specifically — the swing factor between the base and stress scenarios.
5. **Reserve adequacy / override politics.** Closing a 5-10% structural gap eventually requires either sustained service cuts or Prop 2½ overrides; the latter is politically uncertain in a city with Worcester's tax-base profile.

**Mitigants/strengths:** disciplined, ahead-of-schedule pension funding; declining existing debt service; investment-grade stable ratings; a diversified and growing tax base; and the upcoming pension-cliff dividend that *could* be steered into OPEB.

---

## 7. Bottom line

Worcester is **fiscally well-managed in the near term** — balanced, well-rated, and notably ahead of peers on pensions. The risk is entirely **structural and long-dated**: a ~1 point/yr wedge between capped revenue and sticky, education-and-benefit-driven spending that compounds into a **~$67M (5-yr) → ~$160M (10-yr) → ~$480M (20-yr) annual gap** in the base case, with **OPEB** as the dominant unfunded overhang. The city's two best levers — the ~FY2034 pension-funding dividend and declining debt service — can offset roughly half the near-term gap *if deliberately captured*. Whether the 10- and 20-year picture is "manageable with overrides" or "chronic retrenchment" depends mostly on **state aid after the SOA** and on **whether Worcester pivots its pension discipline onto OPEB.**
